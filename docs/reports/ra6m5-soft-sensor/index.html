Fitting a soft sensor

The RA6M5 board runs a closed-loop fan controller that regulates the accelerator's estimated die temperature, and it needs one temperature to do it: a thermocouple bonded between the heatsink fins. This page is about replacing that sensor with a model — so the loop still works on a board where the probe was never fitted, or where it fails mid-run. Four runs went into it: two to characterise the plant, one to fit on, and two held back to test the result on data the fit never saw. The finished model adds exactly — parameter to what the controller already knew, and predicts the probe it replaces to — on held-out data.

Step 1 — how well does the fan cool? (the 59-level ladder)

The plant is heatsink − air = W × Rconv(duty): the heatsink sits above ambient by the card's watts times a thermal resistance the fan controls. Walking the fan from 0 to 100 % in 59 steps, five minutes each, measures that resistance — but only where the card is not throttling.

Convective resistance against fan duty

Each marker is one 5-minute level, read over its last 60 s. Blue is usable data. Orange is the low-duty end where the card was pinned on its thermal limit, so the heatsink reading there is set by the limit and not by the fan — it looks like a continuation of the curve and is not one. The dashed green line is the nine-node table the firmware ended up carrying, which is why it departs from the orange markers rather than following them.

Step 2 — is there a load axis at all? (eight models)

The ladder measures Rconv at one load, so nothing in it tests whether the plant is really linear in watts. Before fitting anything, it was worth knowing whether this card's eight models even draw different power — on the day this ran, only ResNet-50 had ever been benchmarked on it, and every other figure was an estimate from GOP×fps.

Every model's own warm-up, one panel each

Heatsink rise above board air, on a clock that restarts with each 5-minute tread — so treads taken an hour apart lie on top of each other. Identical axes on all eight, which is the point: the shape is the same every time and only the height changes. Fan pinned at 73 % throughout, so the only thing differing between panels is the load.

Step 3 — the fitting run, and the assumption it demolished

24 treads: four fixed fan duties, and within each a ladder of frame-rate caps. The intent was a grid of matched loads at different duties, using the cap to put a chosen wattage at a low fan speed — the one thing swapping models cannot do. The cap did not cooperate.

Step 4 — the model

Three lines. The first two are what the feedforward controller already computed; only the third is new.

What the board runs

tc_ss   = air + W × R_conv(duty)        the steady state the plant is heading for
tau     = R_conv(duty) × C              the time constant, from the same curve
d tc/dt = (tc_ss − tc) / tau            one state: the model's memory of the heatsink

So the entire addition is C, a lumped heatsink heat capacity, fitted jointly with the curve. There is no neural network here and deliberately so: this is monotone in duty by construction, has four numbers a person can check, and runs in a handful of float operations on a 250 ms tick.
