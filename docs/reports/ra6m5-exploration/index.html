RA6M5 probe board exploration

Bringing up a SparkFun Thing Plus RA6M5 as a smart telemetry probe for M.2 edge-AI cards: rail power, airflow, a PWM fan with tachometer and a thermocouple input, then using it to find out how much cooling the card needs. Two 353-minute unattended runs on one rig, each with its fan ramp written into the automation plan itself, and only the card changed between them — a Hailo-8 and then a DeepX DX-M1 in the same M.2 slot, which is what makes the two sets of curves comparable. The ramp now walks the fan controller one hardware level at a time, holding each for five minutes, which is what lets the whole curve be read as equilibrium rather than as a lag.

Key findings

28.6 % duty

All the fan the Hailo-8 needs, and the figure reproduced exactly from the earlier run on a schedule six times longer. Below it the clock still dips to 350 MHz; from it the card holds 400 MHz and 1 372 fps for the remaining four and a half hours.

73 % duty

Where the fan stops. Speed and airflow saturate at 4 590 rpm and 3.35 m/s, and the 17 levels above it move neither — ±5 rpm. Both runs agree on the level. A quarter of this ladder is dead travel.

107 → 59.6 °C

Hailo-8 die across the ramp, both ends now genuinely settled: a 30-minute fan-off soak against a five-minute tread at full fan. The floor arrives at 73 % duty with the fan.

0.78 W leakage

The same 1 372 fps costs 5.15 W at 99 °C and 4.37 W at 59 °C — 19.5 mW/°C, 15.2 % of card power, for identical work. Measured over 14 474 full-rate samples.

246 → 314 fps/W

Hailo-8 hot against cooled, at depth 8: cooling is worth +27.6 % in frames per watt. Both the frame rate and the power move in your favour.

−17 vs −6 % fps

What each card loses to its own soak plateau: the Hailo-8 in discrete clock steps (400→350→300 MHz), the DX-M1 in a fast shallow dither. The Hailo recovers at 28.6 % duty, the DX-M1 at 22.2 %.

59.6 & 62.9 °C

Where the two cards end up with the fan at full, from plateaus 18 °C apart (107 and 89). Two heatsinks, two power levels, 3.3 °C apart at the bottom — and the 30-minute cool-down now shows that floor is ~31 °C of the load's own heat, not the rig running out of air.

1.0 vs ~30 %

The DX-M1 throttles and its clock column still hides it. Over 45 minutes above 88 °C an instantaneous 1 Hz read caught 800 MHz in 26 samples of 2 692; the frame rate says the card was there about thirty times as often. Judge throttling from fps, never from the clock.

0.45 % rpm

How closely the fan reproduced across the two runs, level for level, a day apart with the card swapped: all 58 levels in both, mean difference 14 rpm and 0.012 m/s. The rig is a control, which is what licenses attributing the rest to the card.

17.8 → 8.4 °C/W

Hailo-8 thermal resistance, fan off against fan full — against the DX-M1's 11.6 → 7.2 in the same slot with the same fan. The DX-M1's heatsink is a third better in still air; under forced air they close to 15 %.

What was built

The board owns its sensors, samples them far faster than the GUI's 1 Hz tick, and reports per-second mean, minimum and maximum as plain-text lines over USB. The host only parses. Details are in docs/ra6m5-probe.md.

SensorBusFirmwareWhat reaches the app
INA228 power monitorI2C 0x400.1.0Card rail power, voltage, current, energy, charge and monitor-chip temperature, folded onto the Hailo like any other shunt
FS3000-1005 air velocityI2C 0x280.1.0Airflow in m/s on its own Airflow graph
EMC2101 fan controllerI2C 0x4C0.2.0 → 0.4.0Die temperature (0.2.0); fan duty set by a host fan <percent> command, with speed from the tachometer (0.4.0)
MAX31855K thermocoupleSPI, CS D00.3.0Thermocouple temperature on the Temperature graph. Chip detected, no thermocouple fitted yet
  • In the app: fan speed and duty are drawn on the Airflow graph for now, scaled to share its axis (rpm ÷ 1000, duty ÷ 10). Telemetry → Fan sets the duty, starts from whatever the board reports so opening the app never stops a running fan, and logs every change.
  • Flashing takes no button press: tools/flash-ra6m5.sh builds and uploads. Two boards exist, mapped by USB serial number — so a mapping follows the board and has to be re-pointed when the card in front of it changes.
  • Power readings agree with the card's own sensor. The INA228 reads 1.7 % above the Hailo-8's on-board INA231 at idle and 3.3 % above it under load, and voltage × current matches the power register to within 0.0002 %.

The Hailo-8 run

353 minutes of ResNet-50 at async depth 8, driven entirely by config/automation-thermal-hailo.conf: three minutes to settle, then a 320-minute load whose first 30 minutes run with the fan off, then 58 fan levels held five minutes each — 9.5 % to 100 %, one EMC2101 step at a time — and finally 30 minutes with the load off and the fan still at full, still logging. The fan schedule lives in the plan file, so the run is reproducible and the ramp is in the log rather than in somebody's hands. The bottom panel carries the duty the EMC2101 applied on its left axis and the airflow the FS3000 measured on its right, so the air arriving at the card can be read directly against the setting that produced it; the clock panel above it is where this card's throttling is visible as three discrete steps. Hover or tap any panel to read every value at that moment.

Move over the charts to read values
  • The soak behaved exactly as the plan assumed. The die reached 99 % of its plateau in 408 s and held 107.1 °C (106.0–108.0) through the final five minutes of the half hour. The plateau is an oscillation, not a level: the frame rate cycles between 1 028 and 1 374 fps as the clock steps between 300 and 350 MHz, averaging 1 138 over the last ten minutes, so a single sample of it means nothing.
  • The throttle released at 28.6 % duty, 90:00 into the run — the same duty the 125-minute run found. At 27.0 % the clock still dips to 350 MHz (fps 1 200–1 373 within that one tread); at 28.6 % it reports 400 MHz in every sample and the frame rate sits at 1 371–1 373 with the die at 101–103 °C. Over the remaining 9 510 samples — two and a half hours — the frame rate never fell below 1 365, i.e. 99.5 % of full rate. Two runs six times apart in length agree on the number, which is as close to a transferable cooling requirement as this report gets.
  • A quarter of the ramp is dead travel, and now it is measured rather than inferred. Fan speed and airflow saturate at 73.0 % duty (4 590 rpm, 3.35 m/s) and the die bottoms out at 59.4 °C at the same level. The 17 levels above it — 85 minutes of run time — held the fan to ±5 rpm and the die to ±0.3 °C. On this rig a ladder could stop at 75 % and lose nothing.
  • 58 levels, 58 distinct readbacks, zero repeats. The plan asks for 9.5238 % rising by 1.5873 — exactly 1/63, one EMC2101 step — so every tread lands on its own hardware level and none is written twice. That is what the fractional-percent fan path was added for; the earlier run's integer ramp collided on about a third of its requests. The board's FAN_DUTY readback confirms all 59 values, counting the initial zero.
  • Five minutes a tread is long enough to read as equilibrium. Within each of the last 30 treads the die moves by at most 0.3 °C between the first and last second, against the 1-minute treads of the earlier run where it lagged the fan by about a step the whole way up. The thermal-resistance curve below is therefore a curve of settled points rather than of a chase.

Each duty step was held five minutes — several thermal time constants for this card, which reaches 99 % of a step in about 400 s from cold — so the intermediate treads are settled too, not just the two ends. The equilibrium figures quoted elsewhere are the 30-minute fan-off soak and the last 20 minutes of the load.

The DeepX DX-M1 run

The same 353-minute schedule from config/automation-thermal-deepx.conf — byte-identical in every timing key — with the Hailo-8 taken out of the machine and a DeepX DX-M1 put in the same M.2 slot, same probe board (serial …3BA5), same fan in the same place, same USB port, same ambient. The two schedules were written to be identical for exactly this reason, so what differs between the runs is the card and nothing else; the fan panels below are the control that proves it. ResNet-50 async at depth 4, this card's saturation point. Panels are the same as above, with duty and airflow sharing the bottom one.

Move over the charts to read values
  • The plateau is flat, and it takes 2.9× as long to arrive as the Hailo's. The die reaches 99 % of its plateau at 1 190 s against the Hailo's 408, then holds 89.0 °C (88.0–89.0) for the rest of the half hour. That ratio is why the shared schedule soaks for 30 minutes: it is set by the slower card, and a soak sized for the Hailo would have started this card's ramp with its die still drifting upward.
  • It throttles, and the loss is smaller than the 125-minute run measured. At the plateau the frame rate settles at 1 006 fps over the last ten minutes against 1 083 while still warming and 1 067 fully cooled — a 5.7 % deficit, recovered in full once the fan arrives. The earlier run recorded 9.6 %; this one sat at the plateau for longer and lost less, so treat the magnitude as run-dependent and the existence of the throttle as settled.
  • Cooling returns the frames by 22.2 % duty — 1 314 rpm, 1.18 m/s, die 87 °C, 70:00 into the run — and is within 0.1 % of full rate from 23.8 %. It recovers earlier than the Hailo (28.6 %) and recovers gradually where the Hailo's was a clean clock-state change: a shallow dither coming off rather than a discrete level clearing.
  • The die floor is 63 °C and it is reached at 71.4 % duty, within a level of where the fan itself saturates. From there to the end of the load — 95 minutes, 19 treads — every tread reads 63 °C. dxrt-cli reports whole degrees, so that floor is partly the sensor's resolution; what is not resolution-limited is that the top fifth of the ramp demonstrably changes nothing, the same dead travel the Hailo run shows at the same duty.
  • Leakage is a third the share it is on the Hailo. Over 16 052 samples at this card's full rate, power falls 0.29 W across 23.9 °C — 12.1 mW/°C, 5.0 % of card power, against the Hailo's 19.5 mW/°C and 15.2 %. On a 5.5 W card the thermal case for cooling is mostly about frames, not watts.
  • The rail is the one place this card asks for more than the Hailo. Peak 5.87 W against 5.33, and VBUS sags to 3.126 V against 3.160 — both far above the 3.003 V undervoltage floor the INA228's latched detector watches, and it never tripped in either run.

The 30-minute cool-down after the load stops, fan still at 100 %: from 63 °C at the last loaded sample to 45 / 39.2 / 36.0 / 35.0 °C at +90 / +180 / +270 / +300 s, then 31.0 at +10 min and 30.2 °C settled for the last five minutes, with rail power dropping 5.46 → 1.03 W — that idle figure being the floor this card never goes below. The sample one second after the load stops still reads 5.48 W, because the INA228's one-second average there mostly covers the run: the first-sample artifact noted in the footer, mirrored at the other end. The five minutes the earlier plan allowed would have stopped at 35 °C, five degrees above the true floor — which is exactly why the cool-down was lengthened for these runs.

Throttling

The Hailo-8 throttles by stepping its core clock down in discrete levels, and at depth 8 it uses three of them. The frame rate follows the clock almost exactly, so the clock column and the frame rate tell the same story — which, as the DX-M1 shows below, is not something to take for granted.

Core clockShare of the runFrame rate there
400 MHz75.4 %1 372
350 MHz18.8 %1 200
300 MHz5.8 %1 033
  • The throttled plateau is an oscillation, not a level. The clock steps between 300 and 350 MHz and the frame rate cycles 1 028–1 374 with it, averaging 1 138 over the last ten minutes of the soak — 17 % below the 1 372 the same card holds once cooled. A single sample of that plateau means nothing.
  • Three quarters of this run was at full clock, and that is the schedule rather than the card. The shares above are shares of a 320-minute load whose last four and a half hours were fully cooled; on a shorter plan the throttled fraction would be larger. Read them as “what the clock was doing when the card was hot”, not as a duty cycle.
  • Depth matters for where the wall is, not for whether there is one. The 300 MHz step is reached only at depth 8; a depth-4 run on this card tops out at 350. That is not depth 4 being “more thermally robust” — it never runs fast enough to reach the wall, and the die gets just as hot.

The DX-M1 throttles too, and its clock column will not tell you

This project recorded for two weeks that the DX-M1 “does not throttle at all”. This run disproves it, and the reason the claim survived is a sampling artifact worth keeping. The card reports a fixed 1 000 MHz; the host reads that clock with dxrt-cli, which returns an instantaneous value, while the frame rate in the same row is a full one-second average. The two disagree:

Evidence, over the 2 692 s the card sat at 88–89 °CSays the card is at 800 MHz
Clock column _C0/_C1/_C2 — instantaneous, 1 Hz1.0 % of samples (26 of 2 692)
Frame rate — 1 003 against 1 067 fps cooled, a 1 s average~30 % of the time
Only samples reporting a flat 1 000 MHz on all three coresstill 5.9 % slow
  • Almost all of the loss hides inside samples that claim full clock. Restricting to the 2 666 rows where every core reports 1 000 MHz still leaves the card 5.9 % slower hot than cooled — so the dither is faster than one second and a point read cannot resolve it. The 26 samples that do catch 800 MHz are every one a lone one-second blip.
  • The point read got worse with a longer run, which is the clearest sign it is an artifact. The 125-minute run caught 800 MHz in 9.9 % of its plateau samples; this run, sitting at the same temperature twice as long, caught it in 1.0 %. A real duty cycle would not move by an order of magnitude between two runs that agree on the frame rate to within a few percent.
  • Use the frame rate as the throttle indicator on this card. The clock column is honest about the instant it samples and misleading about the interval, which is the opposite of what a thermal investigation needs.
  • And never conclude “no throttle” from a run that stayed below the knee. The original 23.7-minute run was not wrong about its own data — fps really did sit at 1 030–1 090 for its whole length. It just stopped a few minutes after the die arrived at the plateau, and the throttle needs to be sat at, not merely reached.
  • The magnitude is not stable between runs, so quote it with its window. 9.6 % on the 125-minute run, 5.7 % here. Same card, same slot, same plateau temperature, same model, same depth — so whatever sets the dither's duty cycle is not captured by any of those, and “the DX-M1 loses about 6–10 % at 89 °C” is the honest statement.

The dither also makes the DX-M1's plateau an oscillation, like the Hailo's, so a single sample of it means as little here as there — for a different underlying reason, and with a tenth of the amplitude.

Hot silicon costs power

Rail power against die temperature, each card on its own axis. The work is constant within each run, yet power climbs steadily with temperature — this is leakage, and it is a property of the silicon rather than of the configuration. Measured over the Hailo-8's 14 474 identical-1 372 fps samples it comes to 19.5 mW/°C, or 0.78 W across a 40 °C span — 4.37 W at 59.3 °C against 5.15 W at 99.4 °C, 15.2 % of the card's power, bought back purely by cooling it. Any fps/W figure for either card is meaningless without its die temperature.

Hailo-8  depth 8 (~1 372 fps cooled)
DeepX DX-M1  depth 4 (~1 067 fps cooled)

The DX-M1 has a similar slope and a far smaller share. Over its own 16 052 full-rate samples power falls 0.29 W across 23.9 °C — 12.1 mW/°C, about 60 % of the Hailo's rate and, on a 5.5 W card instead of a 4.4 W one, 5.0 % of card power against 15.2 %. Normalising matters here: the DX-M1's raw power barely moves across the ramp (5.56 W at the soak plateau, 5.46 W fully cooled) because it is also recovering 6 % of its frame rate as it cools, and the extra work very nearly cancels the leakage saved. Read those two numbers side by side without accounting for the frame rate and the card looks temperature-independent, which it is not.

Power efficiency

Frames per watt for every sample of each run, against die temperature. At full clock the frame rate is flat, so efficiency falls only because power rises — the Hailo-8 from 325 fps/W at its coolest loaded sample to 247 above 107 °C. Throttling does not recover it: a throttled card draws less power but loses frames in at least the same proportion, so it lands on or below the same curve. There is no efficiency to be had by letting either card get hot.

The DX-M1 needs its own axis, because its efficiency band (170–205 fps/W) does not overlap the Hailo's at all — it is a 5.5 W card doing about 1 067 fps where the Hailo does 1 372 on 4.4 W. The fan

Noctua NF-A4x10 5V PWM, the same fan in the same position for both runs. Each point is the mean of a full held five minutes at each of 58 levels — every EMC2101 step from the lowest that turns the fan to the highest, with none repeated. The duty axis is the value the controller actually applied; the plan asks in exact 1/63 increments so request and readback agree to four decimals. The two curves are plotted together as a control: the runs are a day apart with the card swapped between them, so any difference here would undermine every comparison in the report.

  • The two curves are indistinguishable, and this is now a 58-point control with five-minute means. Level for level, the mean difference is 14 rpm — 0.45 % — and 0.012 m/s, 0.48 %, with no level more than 32 rpm or 0.026 m/s apart (the worst is 17.5 % duty, 0.866 against 0.840 m/s). A day apart, with the card pulled and replaced in between. The fan and the airflow sensor are a known quantity, reproducible to better than half a percent, which is what licenses attributing everything else in this report to the card.
  • The ladder starts at 9.5 % because the earlier run established that is where the fan begins to turn. Below it the EMC2101's spin-up drive kicks the rotor and it stalls — 4.8 % kicks and stops, 6.3 % never turns at all, 7.9 % oscillates on a ten-second cycle with a quarter of its samples at rest — all of it reproduced level for level across both of the 125-minute runs. Those levels are deliberately not swept here: a duty the fan will not hold is not an operating point, and sweeping it only wastes treads. Read the tachometer, never the duty.
  • Speed rises almost linearly to about 71 % duty — 66 rpm per point over 11.1–71.4 % — and then stops dead. From 73.0 % the fan is within 1 % of its maximum, and over the 17 levels from 74.6 % upward it holds 4 587 ± 5 rpm in the Hailo run and 4 606 ± 3 in the DX-M1 run. That is 85 minutes of each run spent changing nothing.
  • Airflow saturates with the fan, at 3.35 m/s. The FS3000 tracks rpm all the way up and flattens at the same level, so the dead travel is not a sensor ceiling — the air genuinely stops increasing. Anyone sizing this rig should treat ~73 % duty as the top of the useful range and spend the rest of the ladder elsewhere.
  • Both cards reach their die floor within two levels of the fan's own ceiling — the Hailo at 73.0 %, the DX-M1 at 71.4 %. The cooling limit at the top of this ladder is therefore the fan's, not either heatsink's, and the cool-down section below is what separates those two explanations.

  • This is the comparison the whole rig exists to make, and unlike frame rate it needs no normalising: fps/W is frames per joule, so the two cards can share one axis directly. Same model, same host, same slot, same shunt, same fan.
  • The two bands do not overlap anywhere. Over all 19 198 benchmarked samples of each run, spanning 43–108 °C, the DX-M1 ranges 169.7–204.8 fps/W and the Hailo-8 214.8–325.3 — the DX-M1's best sample still falls 10.0 fps/W short of the Hailo's worst. The margin is almost exactly what the shorter runs measured, on 2.6× the samples.
  • Cooled, the gap is 1.60×; soaked, 1.36×. Hailo 313.7 against DX-M1 195.5 fps/W with the fan at full; 245.8 against 181.0 at the fan-off plateau. In energy terms the Hailo does the same frame for 3.19 mJ against 5.12 — 62 % of the energy — and 4.07 against 5.53 when both are left to cook.
  • The better card is also the one with more to gain from air. Cooling is worth +27.6 % to the Hailo's efficiency and only +8.0 % to the DX-M1's, because the Hailo loses on both terms at once — 17 % of its frame rate to clock throttling and 15.2 % of its power to leakage, where the DX-M1 loses 5.7 % and 5.0 %. So poor cooling narrows the gap between these cards: neglect the airflow and you give away most of the Hailo's advantage.
  • The shapes differ as much as the levels. On the time plot the Hailo's trace is visibly noisy through the soak — the clock oscillating between 300 and 350 MHz moves fps and power together — then steps up sharply as the fan passes 20–30 % duty, and is dead flat for the last four and a half hours. The DX-M1 climbs gently and almost monotonically across the whole ramp. Against temperature both are near-linear, with the Hailo's slope about 3× the DX-M1's.

The first running sample of each run is excluded: the INA228's one-second average there still covers mostly idle and reports a fps/W several times too high — the artifact noted in the footer. Samples outside the benchmark are excluded too, since fps/W on an idle rail is not an efficiency.

They narrow because the Hailo starts so much worse in still air, not because they are being squeezed against a common ceiling; the next section shows there is no such ceiling in reach.

Where the cooling limit sits

With the fan and the airflow sensor proved reproducible to 0.5 %, the two runs can be read as one experiment with the card as its only variable. Thermal resistance below is the card die above the EMC2101's reading on the probe board (air near the card), divided by INA228 rail power — °C of die above local ambient per watt dissipated. Using a local ambient rather than a room temperature is what makes two runs on different days comparable. Every figure here is now taken from a genuinely settled window: the 30-minute fan-off soak at one end, the last 20 minutes of a 320-minute load at the other, and five-minute treads in between.

  • In still air the cards differ by a third, and that is the heatsink. The DX-M1 sheds 11.6 °C/W against the Hailo's 17.8 with no air moving at all — so it reaches only 88.7 °C while dissipating more power (5.56 W against 4.63). With the rig held fixed this is as close to a property of the card alone as anything here gets.
  • The 30-minute cool-down answers the question the earlier report had to leave open, and the answer is that the rig is not the limit. With the load off and the fan still at 100 %, each die falls to within 5.3 °C (Hailo) and 7.1 °C (DX-M1) of the probe board's own air sensor. The air path is nowhere near saturated at 60 °C; it will take a die to a few degrees above local air as soon as the heat stops. That is the whole value of spending the end delay logging rather than winding down.
  • The loaded floor is just power × the card's own resistance, with nothing left over. Hailo: 23.0 °C of board air + 4.375 W × 8.36 °C/W = 59.5 °C predicted against 59.6 measured. DX-M1: 23.1 + 5.457 × 7.21 = 62.4 against 62.9. No external ceiling is needed to explain either number, which is the quantitative form of the previous point.
  • So the earlier run's “shared floor” was a coincidence, and this report retracts it. That reading rested on the two cards landing 0.8 °C apart (63.2 and 64.0) after one-minute treads and a 20-minute window. With both ends properly settled they land 3.3 °C apart and in the opposite order — 59.6 for the Hailo, 62.9 for the DX-M1 — exactly as their different power and different resistance predict. Two cards still cannot separate every hypothesis, but they no longer need to: the cool-down measures the air path directly.
  • The fan is worth less on the better-cooled card. It buys the Hailo 2.12× and the DX-M1 1.60×, because the DX-M1 starts so much closer to its own floor. The card with the worse heatsink has more to gain from air, and still cannot passively reach what the better one does.
  • Both pay for being hot, in different currencies. Against its own cooled rate the Hailo loses 17 % of its frame rate and 15.2 % of its power to leakage; the DX-M1 loses 5.7 % of its frame rate and 5.0 % to leakage. Neither is a card to run without air if either number matters.
  • What this still does not establish. These are die-to-local-air numbers for two different packages, and where each vendor puts its sensor inside the package is uncontrolled — so the 14 % gap at full fan is a measurement of two instruments as much as of two heatsinks. Moving the fan is still a worthwhile experiment, but it is no longer the one this report hinges on: it would refine the air path, not decide between two explanations of the floor.

    • The two cards' die sensors are not the same instrument — the Hailo reports tenths from TS0/TS1, the DX-M1 whole degrees from three sensors via dxrt-cli — so every figure here is taken as the hottest sensor on each card, averaged over a deliberately flat 10-to-30-minute window. What is genuinely uncontrolled is where each vendor puts its sensor inside the package: these are die-to-local-air numbers for two different packages, not two heatsinks on one thermal test chip.

Lessons from the bench

  • Re-plugging the sensor chain needs a rescan. The firmware looks for sensors only at power-up, so sensors connected later stay absent until the host sends rescan.
  • Restart the app after re-flashing or unplugging the board. The app does not reconnect, so readings go blank three seconds after the board drops off.
  • Nothing else may hold the serial port when the app starts. The app sends info and gives the board three seconds to answer; a shell or script reading the same /dev/ttyACM* makes that fail, and the startup note then says no answer to info within 3 s — no shunt, no airflow and, worse, no fan, so an automated ramp runs its whole length doing nothing. Check that note before trusting a long run. The board exposes two CDC nodes and only the first answers.
  • Re-map the shunt after moving the card. The RA6M5 is keyed by USB serial in config/ina228.conf, so the mapping follows the board, not the card it is wired to. Swap the M.2 card without editing that line and the shunt folds onto a card that is no longer present — which accel_present() then hides — while the card that is there silently reports no power at all, and every fps/W in the run is blank.
  • Hold each step about three time constants if you want equilibrium — and this run is what that costs. The 125-minute plan's one-minute treads read as a clean curve and were not one: the die lagged the fan by roughly a step the whole way up. Five minutes a tread fixes it, and the die now moves by at most 0.3 °C within a tread — at the price of a 353-minute run. It was worth it for the thermal-resistance curve and unnecessary for finding where the throttle clears, which the short plan already had right.
  • Find the actuator's ceiling before writing a ladder across its whole range. 17 of these 58 treads — 85 minutes of each run — were spent above the duty where this fan stops responding, and they produced 17 identical rows. One short rpm-versus-duty sweep beforehand would have bought that time back. Read the tachometer, not the setting.
  • Spend the end-of-plan delay logging, not winding down. The 30 minutes after the load stops, with the fan still at full, is what turned “the two cards converge, so the rig is probably the limit” into a measured answer. It costs nothing but wall-clock time and it is the only part of the run where the cooling is observed with the heat switched off. Five minutes was not enough: the DX-M1 was still 5 °C above its floor at +300 s.
  • Check the startup note before committing six hours. It is the one line that says which card the shunt folded onto, which sensors answered, and which accelerators are hidden. An earlier session nearly ran without power telemetry for exactly this reason: the board was still mapped to the Hailo that had been removed, accel_present() hid that absent card, the shunt folded onto it, and the DX-M1's watts would have been blank for the whole run with no error anywhere. The note said INA228 -> DeepX once it was fixed, and that is cheap to read.
  • An instantaneous sample is not an interval measurement. The DX-M1's clock column is read per tick and reports the clock at that instant; the frame rate in the same row averages a whole second. When a card dithers faster than the sample rate the two tell different stories, and the point read is the one that lies — it found 800 MHz in 9.9 % of samples where the frame rate implies 41 %. Prefer the averaged quantity for anything about how much of the time, and be suspicious of a per-tick reading that disagrees with a rate.
  • A null result from a short run is not a null result. “The DX-M1 does not throttle” stood on a 23.7-minute run that was perfectly accurate about itself and stopped minutes after the die reached its plateau. The throttle needed the plateau to be sat at for half an hour before it appeared. Before recording an absence, check the run was long enough for the thing to have shown up.
  • Put the fan schedule in the plan file, not in a side script. The ramp is part of the experiment, so it belongs where the rest of the run is specified and where the log can be read back against it. fan, fan.start, fan.step, fan.every, fan.after and fan.max now do this; a plan that says nothing about the fan deliberately leaves it alone, since the firmware keeps its duty across host disconnects.

Still open

  • Fit the thermocouple — it is now the instrument this work needs. The MAX31855K is detected and reads its cold junction, but reports an open circuit with no probe attached, so its column is empty in both logs (0 valid readings of 21 179 in each). Bonded to the Hailo's heatsink it would measure air actually arriving at the card, which is precisely the quantity the previous section has to infer from die temperature and rail power. It is the cheapest way to turn “the airflow sensor cannot see the difference” into a direct reading.
  • A second airflow sensor, if needed. The FS3000's address is fixed and the board has one I2C bus, so a second sensor would need an I2C mux (TCA9548A).
  • The per-second minimum and maximum that the board sends are not used by the app yet.
  • A fan graph of its own. Fan rpm and duty are still drawn on the Airflow graph, scaled to share its axis; separating them is a MainWindow-only change.
  • Per-metric suffixes in the INA228 fold, so the per-second minimum and maximum the board already sends could reach the host without colliding with the folded <Card> INA228 <FAMILY> column names.
  • Shorten the ladder and spend the time elsewhere. Nothing above 73 % duty moved anything in either run, so a plan stopping there — 41 treads instead of 58 — would run a 235-minute load instead of 320 and lose no information. The 85 minutes saved would pay for holding the two treads either side of each card's throttle-release point for half an hour each, which is where the remaining uncertainty actually is.
  • Move the fan, then re-run one of these plans unchanged. Still worth doing, but demoted: the cool-down now measures the air path directly and shows it is not the limit under load, so this would refine a known quantity rather than decide an open question. A second fan is the obvious variant.
  • Resolve the DX-M1's dither directly — it is now the largest unexplained number here. The ~30 % figure is inferred from the frame rate on the assumption that throughput is linear in clock, and the inferred duty cycle moved from 41 % to 30 % between two runs while the point-read moved from 9.9 % to 1.0 %. Sampling dxrt-cli far faster than 1 Hz during a soak would measure it instead of inferring it, and would confirm 800 MHz is the only step the card uses.
  • Run the remaining two cards through the same plan. config/automation-thermal-memryx.conf and -axelera.conf now exist with the same 353-minute schedule, and neither card has been characterised this way. Both need care: the MX3 wedges at async depth 8, which is its saturation point, and the Metis has an open host-freeze investigation of its own. A 320-minute load is a long time to hold either.
