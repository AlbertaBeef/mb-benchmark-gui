RA6M5 probe board exploration

Bringing up a SparkFun Thing Plus RA6M5 as a smart telemetry probe for M.2 edge-AI cards: rail power, airflow, a PWM fan with tachometer and a thermocouple input, then using it to find out how much cooling the card needs. Two 125-minute unattended runs on one rig, each with its fan ramp written into the automation plan itself, and only the card changed between them — a Hailo-8 and then a DeepX DX-M1 in the same M.2 slot, which is what makes the two sets of curves comparable.

16–19 Nov 2026Host: AMD EPYC ROMED8-2TCards: Hailo-8 M.2 (depth 8) · DeepX DX-M1 (depth 4)ResNet-50 async · Firmware: mb-probe-ra6m5 0.4.0

Key findings

28.6 % duty

All the fan the card needs. The throttle released in a single sample — 1 201 → 1 359 fps at 99 °C — and never returned over the next 70 minutes. Everything above this is margin.

107 → 63 °C

Hailo-8 die temperature across the ramp: 30 minutes of fan-off soak against the last 20 minutes above 87 % duty. The floor arrives at 75 % duty; the top quarter of the ramp changes nothing.

0.77 W leakage

The same 1 372 fps costs 5.19 W at 100 °C and 4.41 W at 63 °C — 20.6 mW/°C, 15 % of card power, for identical work.

246 → 311 fps/W

Hot against cooled, at depth 8: cooling is worth 27 % more frames per watt. Both the frame rate and the power move in your favour.

−18 vs −8 % fps

What each card loses to its own soak plateau: the Hailo-8 in discrete clock steps (400→350→300 MHz, 39 % of samples), the DX-M1 in a fast shallow dither. Both recover fully below 30 % duty.

63 & 64 °C

Where both cards end up with the fan at full, from plateaus 18 °C apart (107 and 89). Two different heatsinks, two different power levels, one floor — and it is the rig's, not either card's.

9.9 vs 41 %

The DX-M1 does throttle, and its clock column hides it. An instantaneous 1 Hz read catches 800 MHz in a tenth of the samples; the frame rate says the card is there four times as often. Judge throttling from fps, never from the clock.

11.9 → 7.3 °C/W

The DX-M1 on the same rig, fan off against fan full — against the Hailo's 17.9 → 8.9 in the same slot with the same fan. The DX-M1's heatsink is far better in still air and the two converge under forced air.

What was built

The board owns its sensors, samples them far faster than the GUI's 1 Hz tick, and reports per-second mean, minimum and maximum as plain-text lines over USB. The host only parses. Details are in docs/ra6m5-probe.md.

SensorBusFirmwareWhat reaches the app
INA228 power monitorI2C 0x400.1.0Card rail power, voltage, current, energy, charge and monitor-chip temperature, folded onto the Hailo like any other shunt
FS3000-1005 air velocityI2C 0x280.1.0Airflow in m/s on its own Airflow graph
EMC2101 fan controllerI2C 0x4C0.2.0 → 0.4.0Die temperature (0.2.0); fan duty set by a host fan <percent> command, with speed from the tachometer (0.4.0)
MAX31855K thermocoupleSPI, CS D00.3.0Thermocouple temperature on the Temperature graph. Chip detected, no thermocouple fitted yet
  • In the app: fan speed and duty are drawn on the Airflow graph for now, scaled to share its axis (rpm ÷ 1000, duty ÷ 10). Telemetry → Fan sets the duty, starts from whatever the board reports so opening the app never stops a running fan, and logs every change.
  • Flashing takes no button press: tools/flash-ra6m5.sh builds and uploads. Two boards exist, mapped by USB serial number — so a mapping follows the board and has to be re-pointed when the card in front of it changes.
  • Power readings agree with the card's own sensor. The INA228 reads 1.7 % above the Hailo-8's on-board INA231 at idle and 3.3 % above it under load, and voltage × current matches the power register to within 0.0002 %.

The Hailo-8 run

125 minutes of ResNet-50 at async depth 8, driven entirely by config/automation-thermal-hailo.conf: 30 minutes with the fan off, then 5 % rising one point a minute to 100 %. The fan schedule lives in the plan file, so the run is reproducible and the ramp is in the log rather than in somebody's hands. The bottom panel carries the duty the EMC2101 applied on its left axis and the airflow the FS3000 measured on its right, so the air arriving at the card can be read directly against the setting that produced it. Hover or tap any panel to read every value at that moment.

Move over the charts to read values
  • The soak behaved exactly as the plan assumed. The die reached its plateau by about 8 minutes and held 107.1 °C (105.9–108.1) for the rest of the half hour. The plateau is an oscillation, not a level: the frame rate cycles between 1 029 and 1 201 fps as the clock steps between 300 and 350 MHz, so a single sample of it means nothing.
  • The throttle released at 28.6 % duty, 53:48 into the run, in one sample: 1 201 → 1 359 fps with the die at 99 °C. Over the remaining 70 minutes the frame rate never fell below 1 359 again.
  • The last quarter of the ramp is dead travel. Fan speed and airflow saturate at 74.6 % duty (4 611 rpm, 3.36 m/s) and the die bottoms out at 63.2 °C at the same point. The 24 duty steps above it changed neither.
  • 62 of about 95 one-point requests actually moved the fan. The EMC2101 quantises duty to 64 levels, 1.587 % apart, so 5–100 % spans just 61 distinct levels — and the 7 % and 10 % requests are visible in the log as exact no-ops against 6 % and 9 %. Always read the board's FAN_DUTY readback, never the percent requested.
  • The fan kicks before it can hold, and the airflow trace shows it. The first 4.8 % step produced two moving samples, peaking at 1 102 rpm and 0.385 m/s — the EMC2101's spin-up drive breaking static friction — then stalled; 6.3 % never turned it at all (120 samples, 0 rpm). At 7.9 % it oscillates: a kick to ~2 500 rpm and 2.0 m/s, a six-second decay, then stall, on a ~10-second cycle — 25 of that level's 60 samples are at rest. Continuous rotation starts at 9.5 % (364 rpm, 0.16 m/s) and the clean monotonic part of the curve at 11.1 %.

Each duty step was held for one minute, which is roughly one thermal time constant for this card, so the die lags the fan by about a step through the ramp. The two figures quoted as equilibrium — the 30-minute soak and the final 20 minutes — are genuinely flat; the intermediate steps are not.

The DeepX DX-M1 run

The same 125-minute schedule from config/automation-thermal-deepx.conf, with the Hailo-8 taken out of the machine and a DeepX DX-M1 put in the same M.2 slot — same probe board (serial …3BA5), same fan in the same place, same USB port, same ambient. The two schedules were written to be identical for exactly this reason, so what differs between the runs is the card and nothing else. ResNet-50 async at depth 4, this card's saturation point. Panels are the same as above, with duty and airflow sharing the bottom one.

Move over the charts to read values
  • The soak question the plan header raised is settled. That header warned that 30 minutes took the card about 6 minutes past the longest run ever recorded on it, so the last stretch was unmeasured. It is flat: the die reached 89.0 °C by about 20 minutes and held 88.0–89.0 for the final five, with the plateau arriving near 1 200 s rather than the 1 000 estimated from the shorter run.
  • It throttles — which this project had recorded as not happening. At the plateau the frame rate settles at 979 fps against 1 083 while still warming, a 9.6 % loss held for the whole half hour and recovered in full once the fan arrives. The earlier conclusion came from a 23.7-minute run that never sat at the plateau long enough. See the next section for why the clock column does not show it.
  • Cooling returns the frames. The frame rate climbs out of the throttle from 20.6 % duty and is back to its full 1 070 by 28.6 %, then flat for the remaining 70 minutes. The recovery is gradual where the Hailo's was a single-sample step — a shallow dither coming off rather than a discrete clock state clearing.
  • The die floor is 64 °C and it is reached at 79 % duty. Over the 21 minutes from there to the end of the ramp the reading is 64 °C in 1 254 of 1 259 samples, with five at 63. dxrt-cli reports whole degrees, so that floor is partly the sensor's resolution — but the top fifth of the ramp demonstrably changes nothing, the same dead travel session 2 showed.
  • Leakage is a quarter the share it is on the Hailo. Normalised to equal frame rate, power falls 0.19 W across 17.3 °C — 10.8 mW/°C, 3.4 % of card power, against the Hailo's 20.6 mW/°C and 15 %. On a 5.5 W card the thermal case for cooling is about frames, not watts.
  • The fan cannot hold its lowest useful setting. At 7.9 % duty the tachometer shows a repeating ~10-second cycle — kicked to 2 500 rpm by the spin-up drive, decaying to 85 rpm, kicked again. It only settles into steady (if slow) rotation at 9.5 %, at 364 rpm. The Hailo run did the same thing at the same duty — 25 of 60 samples at rest in both, mean airflow 0.416 against 0.419 m/s — so this is static friction, and it is reproducible rather than a quirk of one run.

The 5-minute cool-down after the load stops, fan still at 100 %: from 64 °C at the last loaded sample to 46 / 41 / 37 / 36 °C at +90 / +180 / +270 / +300 s, with rail power dropping 5.48 → 1.05 W — that idle figure being the floor this card never goes below. The sample one second after the load stops still reads 5.42 W, because the INA228's one-second average there mostly covers the run: the first-sample artifact noted in the footer, mirrored at the other end.

Throttling

The Hailo-8 throttles by stepping its core clock down in discrete levels, and at depth 8 it uses three of them. The frame rate follows the clock almost exactly, so the clock column and the frame rate tell the same story — which, as the DX-M1 shows below, is not something to take for granted.

Core clockShare of the runFrame rate there
400 MHz60.9 %1 372
350 MHz26.6 %1 201
300 MHz12.6 %1 029
  • The throttled plateau is an oscillation, not a level. The clock steps between 300 and 350 MHz and the frame rate cycles 1 029–1 201 with it, averaging 1 123 — 18 % below the 1 372 the same card holds once cooled. A single sample of that plateau means nothing.
  • Depth matters for where the wall is, not for whether there is one. The 300 MHz step is reached only at depth 8; a depth-4 run on this card tops out at 350. That is not depth 4 being “more thermally robust” — it never runs fast enough to reach the wall, and the die gets just as hot.

The DX-M1 throttles too, and its clock column will not tell you

This project recorded for two weeks that the DX-M1 “does not throttle at all”. This run disproves it, and the reason the claim survived is a sampling artifact worth keeping. The card reports a fixed 1 000 MHz; the host reads that clock with dxrt-cli, which returns an instantaneous value, while the frame rate in the same row is a full one-second average. The two disagree:

Evidence, over the 1 312 s the card sat at 88–89 °CSays the card is at 800 MHz
Clock column _C0/_C1/_C2 — instantaneous, 1 Hz9.9 % of samples (130 of 1 312)
Frame rate — 979 against 1 083 fps, a 1 s average~41 % of the time
Only samples reporting a flat 1 000 MHz on all three coresstill 8.1 % slow
  • Almost all of the loss hides inside samples that claim full clock. Restricting to rows where every core reports 1 000 MHz still leaves the card 8.1 % slower hot than cold — so the dither is faster than one second and a point read cannot resolve it. The 130 samples that do catch 800 MHz are every one a lone one-second blip.
  • Use the frame rate as the throttle indicator on this card. The clock column is honest about the instant it samples and misleading about the interval, which is the opposite of what a thermal investigation needs.
  • And never conclude “no throttle” from a run that stayed below the knee. The original 23.7-minute run was not wrong about its own data — fps really did sit at 1 030–1 090 for its whole length. It just stopped a few minutes after the die arrived at the plateau, and the throttle needs to be sat at, not merely reached.

The dither also makes the DX-M1's plateau an oscillation, like the Hailo's, so a single sample of it means as little here as there — for a different underlying reason, and with a tenth of the amplitude.

Hot silicon costs power

Rail power against die temperature, each card on its own axis. The work is constant within each run, yet power climbs steadily with temperature — this is leakage, and it is a property of the silicon rather than of the configuration. Measured on the Hailo-8's identical 1 372 fps samples it comes to 20.6 mW/°C, or 0.77 W across the run's 37 °C span: 15 % of the card's power, bought back purely by cooling it. Any fps/W figure for either card is meaningless without its die temperature.

Hailo-8  depth 8 (~1 372 fps cooled)
DeepX DX-M1  depth 4 (~1 067 fps cooled)

The DX-M1 has the same slope and a far smaller share. Normalised to equal frame rate its power falls 0.19 W over 17.3 °C — 10.8 mW/°C, half the Hailo's rate and, on a 5.5 W card instead of a 4.4 W one, 3.4 % of card power against 15 %. Normalising matters here: the DX-M1's raw power barely moves across the ramp (5.44 W hot, 5.46 W cold) because it is also recovering 9 % of its frame rate as it cools, and the extra work very nearly cancels the leakage saved. Read those two numbers side by side without accounting for the frame rate and the card looks temperature-independent, which it is not.

Power efficiency

Frames per watt for every sample of each run, against die temperature. At full clock the frame rate is flat, so efficiency falls only because power rises — the Hailo-8 from 322 fps/W at 47 °C to 246 above 105 °C. Throttling does not recover it: a throttled card draws less power but loses frames in at least the same proportion, so it lands on or below the same curve. There is no efficiency to be had by letting either card get hot.

The DX-M1 needs its own axis, because its efficiency band (173–203 fps/W) does not overlap the Hailo's at all — it is a 5.5 W card doing about 1 070 fps where the Hailo does 1 372 on 4.4 W. The Hailo-8 is 60 % more efficient at this model on this rig when both are cooled (311 against 195 fps/W) and 37 % more when both are left to soak (246 against 180) — the gap narrows because the Hailo has the more to lose from heat. The shape is the same and the slope is shallower: 179.8 → 195.4 fps/W, +8.6 % for cooling, against the Hailo's +27 %. The fan

Noctua NF-A4x10 5V PWM, the same fan in the same position for both runs. Each point is the mean of a full held minute at each of 60 levels. The duty axis is the value the EMC2101 actually applied — it has 64 steps, so a 20 % request reads back as 20.6 %. The two curves are plotted together as a control: the runs are a day apart with the card swapped between them, so any difference here would undermine every comparison in the report.

Airflow (m/s) against duty (%)  Hailo-8 runDX-M1 run
  • The two curves are indistinguishable. Over the 56 duty levels both runs reached above 11 %, the mean difference in airflow is 0.011 m/s — 0.4 %, and no step is more than 0.04 m/s apart. A day apart, with the card pulled and replaced in between. The fan and the airflow sensor are a known quantity, reproducible to better than a percent, which is what licenses attributing everything that follows to the card.
  • Rotation needs more duty than the fan controller's first step. The spin-up drive moves it briefly at 4.8 % and it stalls; 6.3 % does not turn it at all; 7.9 % turns it only in bursts. Continuous rotation starts at 9.5 % and the clean monotonic part of the curve at 11.1 %. Do not treat a non-zero duty as a turning fan — read the tachometer.
  • Speed rises almost linearly to about 70 % duty (roughly 65 rpm per point), then flattens: 4 611 rpm and 3.36 m/s from 74.6 % upward in the Hailo run, 4 618 rpm in the DX-M1 run. Settings above 75 % buy nothing measurable — held ±20 and ±4 rpm respectively over the last 40 minutes of each.
  • Even where the fan will not hold, it fails identically in both runs. Level for level: 4.8 % kicks and stalls (58 of 60 samples at rest in both), 6.3 % never turns (120 samples, 0 rpm, in both), 7.9 % oscillates with 25 of 60 samples at rest in each run and mean airflow 0.419 against 0.416 m/s, and both settle into continuous rotation at 9.5 %, at 375 and 372 rpm. An earlier reading of this called the stall region the one unrepeatable part of the fan's behaviour; at full 1 Hz resolution it is the opposite — it reproduces as closely as the rest of the curve. Treat anything below 9.5 % as duty the fan will not hold, and read the tachometer rather than the duty.
  • Why the 7.9 % row of both tables below reads higher than 9.5 %. 637 rpm and 0.42 m/s against 375 rpm and 0.16 — not a non-monotonic fan, but a minute-long mean of kick-and-stall against a minute of steady rotation. Both runs show the same inversion at the same level, which is itself the point: a mean over an unstable duty level is not an operating point.

  • This is the comparison the whole rig exists to make, and unlike frame rate it needs no normalising: fps/W is frames per joule, so the two cards can share one axis directly. Same model, same host, same slot, same shunt, same fan.
  • The two bands do not overlap anywhere.   • The better card is also the one with more to gain from air. Cooling is worth +26.7 % to the Hailo's efficiency and only +8.6 % to the DX-M1's, because the Hailo loses on both terms at once — 18 % of its frame rate to clock throttling and 15 % of its power to leakage, where the DX-M1 loses 8 % and 3.4 %. So poor cooling narrows the gap between these cards: neglect the airflow and you give away most of the Hailo's advantage.
  • The shapes differ as much as the levels. On the time plot the Hailo's trace is visibly noisy through the soak — the clock oscillating between 300 and 350 MHz moves fps and power together — then steps up sharply as the fan passes 20–30 % duty. The DX-M1 climbs gently and almost monotonically across the whole ramp. Against temperature both are near-linear, with the Hailo's slope about 3× the DX-M1's.

Where the cooling limit sits

With the fan and the airflow sensor proved reproducible to 0.4 %, the two runs can be read as one experiment with the card as its only variable. Thermal resistance below is the card die above the EMC2101's reading on the probe board (air near the card), divided by INA228 rail power — °C of die above local ambient per watt dissipated. Using a local ambient rather than a room temperature is what makes two runs on different days comparable.

  • In still air the cards differ by a third, and that is the heatsink. The DX-M1 sheds 11.9 °C/W against the Hailo's 17.9 with no air moving at all — so it reaches only 89 °C while dissipating more power (5.44 W against 4.57). With the rig held fixed this is as close to a property of the card alone as anything here gets.
  • Under forced air they converge, and the floor is suspiciously flat. The gap narrows from 34 % to 18 %, and the two cards settle within 0.8 °C of each other — 63.2 and 64.0 — having started 18 °C apart. Two different heatsinks dissipating different power arriving at the same temperature is what you would expect if the limit under forced air belongs to the rig — how much of the fan's air reaches the slot — rather than to either card.
  • So the fan is worth less on the better-cooled card. It buys the Hailo 2.02× and the DX-M1 only 1.62×, because the DX-M1 starts closer to the floor the rig imposes. The card with the worse heatsink has more to gain from air, and still cannot passively reach what the better one does.
  • Both pay for being hot, in different currencies. Against its own cooled rate the Hailo loses 18 % of its frame rate and 15 % of its power to leakage; the DX-M1 loses 8 % of its frame rate and 3.4 % to leakage. Neither is a card to run without air if either number matters.
  • What this does not establish. The convergence is consistent with a badly coupled fan, and it is not proof: the two runs differ by card, so a shared floor could in principle be two unrelated heatsinks happening to land together. Separating “the rig limits both” from “these two cards coincide” needs one card measured against a changed air path — move the fan, or add a second, and re-run one of these plans unchanged. That is the next experiment, and the schedule being a file makes it a cheap one.

The two cards' die sensors are not the same instrument — the Hailo reports tenths from TS0/TS1, the DX-M1 whole degrees from three sensors via dxrt-cli — so every figure here is taken as the hottest sensor on each card, averaged over a deliberately flat 5-to-20-minute window. What is genuinely uncontrolled is where each vendor puts its sensor inside the package: these are die-to-local-air numbers for two different packages, not two heatsinks on one thermal test chip.

Lessons from the bench

  • Re-plugging the sensor chain needs a rescan. The firmware looks for sensors only at power-up, so sensors connected later stay absent until the host sends rescan.
  • Restart the app after re-flashing or unplugging the board. The app does not reconnect, so readings go blank three seconds after the board drops off.
  • Nothing else may hold the serial port when the app starts. The app sends info and gives the board three seconds to answer; a shell or script reading the same /dev/ttyACM* makes that fail, and the startup note then says no answer to info within 3 s — no shunt, no airflow and, worse, no fan, so an automated ramp runs its whole length doing nothing. Check that note before trusting a long run. The board exposes two CDC nodes and only the first answers.
  • Re-map the shunt after moving the card. The RA6M5 is keyed by USB serial in config/ina228.conf, so the mapping follows the board, not the card it is wired to. Swap the M.2 card without editing that line and the shunt folds onto a card that is no longer present — which accel_present() then hides — while the card that is there silently reports no power at all, and every fps/W in the run is blank.
  • Hold each step about three time constants if you want equilibrium. One minute per step reads as a clean curve and is not one: the die lags the fan by roughly a step throughout. It is fine for finding where the throttle clears, and wrong for a thermal-resistance number — take those from a deliberately flat stretch.
  • Check the startup note before committing two hours. It is the one line that says which card the shunt folded onto, which sensors answered, and which accelerators are hidden. Session 3 nearly ran without power telemetry for exactly this reason: the board was still mapped to the Hailo that had been removed, accel_present() hid that absent card, the shunt folded onto it, and the DX-M1's watts would have been blank for the whole run with no error anywhere. The note said INA228 -> DeepX once it was fixed, and that is cheap to read.
  • An instantaneous sample is not an interval measurement. The DX-M1's clock column is read per tick and reports the clock at that instant; the frame rate in the same row averages a whole second. When a card dithers faster than the sample rate the two tell different stories, and the point read is the one that lies — it found 800 MHz in 9.9 % of samples where the frame rate implies 41 %. Prefer the averaged quantity for anything about how much of the time, and be suspicious of a per-tick reading that disagrees with a rate.
  • A null result from a short run is not a null result. “The DX-M1 does not throttle” stood on a 23.7-minute run that was perfectly accurate about itself and stopped minutes after the die reached its plateau. The throttle needed the plateau to be sat at for half an hour before it appeared. Before recording an absence, check the run was long enough for the thing to have shown up.
  • Put the fan schedule in the plan file, not in a side script. The ramp is part of the experiment, so it belongs where the rest of the run is specified and where the log can be read back against it. fan, fan.start, fan.step, fan.every, fan.after and fan.max now do this; a plan that says nothing about the fan deliberately leaves it alone, since the firmware keeps its duty across host disconnects.

Still open

  • Fit the thermocouple — it is now the instrument this work needs. The MAX31855K is detected and reads its cold junction, but reports an open circuit with no probe attached, so its column is empty in both logs (0 valid readings of 7 979 in each). Bonded to the Hailo's heatsink it would measure air actually arriving at the card, which is precisely the quantity the previous section has to infer from die temperature and rail power. It is the cheapest way to turn “the airflow sensor cannot see the difference” into a direct reading.
  • A second airflow sensor, if needed. The FS3000's address is fixed and the board has one I2C bus, so a second sensor would need an I2C mux (TCA9548A).
  • The per-second minimum and maximum that the board sends are not used by the app yet.
  • A fan graph of its own. Fan rpm and duty are still drawn on the Airflow graph, scaled to share its axis; separating them is a MainWindow-only change.
  • Per-metric suffixes in the INA228 fold, so the per-second minimum and maximum the board already sends could reach the host without colliding with the folded <Card> INA228 <FAMILY> column names.
  • Move the fan, then re-run one of these plans unchanged — this is the experiment with the most to give. Both cards converge on a 7–9 °C/W floor and land within 0.8 °C of each other from 18 °C apart, which points at the air path rather than either heatsink, but two cards cannot separate “the rig limits both” from “these two coincide”. One card against a changed air path can. A second fan is the obvious variant, and nothing above suggests duty over 75 % is worth anything.
  • Resolve the DX-M1's dither directly. The 41 % figure is inferred from the frame rate on the assumption that throughput is linear in clock. Sampling dxrt-cli far faster than 1 Hz during a soak would measure the duty cycle instead of inferring it, and would confirm 800 MHz is the only step it uses.
  • Run the remaining two cards through the same plan. The schedule is now a file, and the MemryX MX3 and Axelera Metis have never been characterised this way. The MX3 would need care — it wedges at async depth 8 and this plan's equivalent saturation point is exactly there.
